Idea brief

The problem

A patient leaves a clinic with a prescription in English and medical shorthand — Tab Telma 40 1-0-0.

If they read Hindi or Marathi more comfortably than English, they depend on a pharmacist or relative to know what each medicine is for and when to take it. They also usually buy the branded medicine on the slip, even when a Jan Aushadhi generic with the same salt costs far less.

  • Households paid 39.4% of India's total health expenditure out of pocket in 2021-22 — National Health Accounts 2021-22, MoHFW.
  • Jan Aushadhi medicines are priced 50–80% below leading brands, and 17,990 Kendras were open by 31 Dec 2025 — Government replies in Parliament.
  • TODO: one sourced statistic on English literacy / prescription understanding, or a real story from the team

Who it's for

Patients and caregivers more comfortable in Hindi or Marathi than English — especially people on long-term medicines for diabetes, blood pressure, thyroid or cholesterol.

The solution

Take a photo of a prescription (or type it). For every medicine MedLens shows, in English, हिन्दी or मराठी:

  • what it's for, how to take it, common side effects and one key warning;
  • when to take it — 1-0-1 x 30 days becomes a morning / afternoon / night chart;
  • an option to hear it read aloud in Hindi or English, for people who find reading hard.

It then finds the Jan Aushadhi medicine with the same salt, strength and form, shows the monthly saving and the nearest Kendra, and makes a WhatsApp list to show at the counter.

Why it can be trusted

  • Explanations come from a fixed reference written per active ingredient — no AI-generated medical text. Review status: TODO: "reviewed by pharmacist NAME" or "not yet reviewed" (the app shows a banner until reviewed)
  • Generic matching is exact: every salt, strength and dosage form must match; sustained-release is never swapped for normal release.
  • Anything uncertain — an unreadable line, a dose written without a unit — is shown as "ask your pharmacist", never guessed.
  • Prescription photos are processed in memory and not stored.

MedLens explains; it does not replace a doctor or pharmacist, and the app says so on every screen.
